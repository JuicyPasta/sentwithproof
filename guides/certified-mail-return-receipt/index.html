Certified Mail and return receipts: what each one proves

When a letter starts a clock or settles an argument, you want to show two things: that you sent it, and that they got it. USPS sells those as separate pieces.

What Certified Mail gives you

  • A mailing receipt with the date you sent it.
  • A tracking number you can look up.
  • A USPS record that the letter was delivered, or that delivery was attempted. USPS keeps the recipient's signature on file.

Certified Mail is an add-on to First-Class Mail and Priority Mail. It does not make the letter travel faster.

What a return receipt adds

Certified Mail alone leaves the signature with USPS. A return receipt sends that proof to you, showing who signed and when. It comes in two forms:

  • Green card (PS Form 3811): a postcard attached to the envelope, signed at delivery and mailed back to you.
  • Electronic return receipt: the same signature and date, delivered as a file. It costs less and can't get lost on the way back.

For most disputes the electronic version is enough. Check whether a contract, lease or statute asks for a specific form before you choose.

What it does not prove

A return receipt proves an envelope was delivered and signed for. It does not prove what was inside. Keep an exact copy of the letter with the receipt, and put the tracking number in the letter itself so the two can be matched.

A Certificate of Mailing is a different, cheaper product. It proves only that you mailed something on a date, with no tracking and no delivery record.

When to send a letter certified

  • Notices to a landlord about repairs, deposits or ending a lease.
  • Demand letters and billing disputes.
  • Cancellations where the company has ignored other channels.
  • Records requests and anything with a response deadline.
  • Any time a contract or law says notice must be sent by certified mail.

USPS fees change every year; the current price list is on usps.com.
